# Memphis — Listen. Feel. Create.

## Why this project exists

Memphis began as one of my first projects: a music app where I could improve the things that frustrated me about listening, from shuffle and navigation to recommendations and personal organization.

The ambition grew beyond playback. I wanted to understand the music itself: who helped make it, why it sounds the way it does, how artists find their own voice, and how songs connect across people, styles and time. That knowledge is scattered across credits, interviews, podcasts, videos and music theory. Following one curiosity can mean hours of searching.

Memphis brings those connections together and gives listeners a way into them. The original desire remains: build a music experience that pays attention to the individual.

## Our goal

Help people understand the music they love, discover music for reasons that matter to them, and develop the confidence to explore and create.

Memphis is a guide to the music universe. Someone should be able to arrive with a favorite song and leave hearing something they had never noticed, knowing more about the people behind it, and wanting to follow the next connection.

## Who we start with

Curious listeners who want to go deeper but do not know where to start. They do not need to read music, know production terminology or aspire to become professional musicians.

The first experience should answer a simple question: “I love this song. Help me understand what I am hearing.”

## The experience we want

**Understand a song.** Explore its sound, structure, instruments, performance and creative context through approachable explanations. Start with what a listener can notice; introduce technical language when it helps.

**Meet the people behind it.** Follow writers, producers, performers and collaborators into their other work. Make contributions visible beyond the main artist name.

**Follow connections.** Move from a song to a technique, a person, a scene or another song. Explain the connection so discovery feels meaningful.

**Find a personal path.** Let listeners save discoveries, organize interests and choose what to explore. Over time, offer recommendations and listening reflections that explain their reasoning and welcome correction.

**Try something creative.** Eventually connect understanding to small experiments: notice how another interpretation changes a melody, compare arrangements, or explore how rhythm changes a song's feeling.

## Questions Memphis could help explore

- In “Die Young,” what might make the music feel continuous or urgent? How might that relate to the lyrical theme?
- Who contributed to a song, in which roles, and where else can we hear their work?
- What makes a Billie Eilish performance distinctive? Which musical traditions or techniques might help describe what we hear beyond a broad genre label?
- How can the same song feel different when someone changes its phrasing, arrangement or instrument?
- Which musical patterns might contribute to a song's appeal, alongside promotion, timing, distribution and cultural context?

These are starting questions for research and listening, not verified claims about particular songs or artists. Memphis should distinguish documented facts, musical observations and interpretations. Shared patterns alone do not explain why a song became successful.

## What makes it personal

A listener is more than a play count or a fixed taste profile. Their interests can change, contradict one another and extend beyond what they already know.

Recommendations should explain why something may interest them and allow both familiarity and surprise. Popularity can provide context, but it is not the goal. A future listening recap should help someone reflect on discoveries and time spent, with clear limits about what their available data can tell us.

Personalization should be optional and transparent. People should control what they share and be able to inspect, correct, export and delete their personal data. Listening history is not a basis for claims about personality or mental health.

## Principles

- Lead with curiosity and clear language; let depth unfold gradually.
- Keep sources close to claims and acknowledge uncertainty or missing information.
- Separate verified facts, editorial interpretations, computed connections and demo content.
- Treat music theory as a way to explore possibilities, not a formula that guarantees a hit.
- Make each connection useful enough to explain in a sentence.
- Keep the core experience useful without hosting music. Link to external listening; use media and data only under permissions that support their intended use.
- Let understanding lead toward creation without making creation a requirement.

## Our first focus

Build one convincing journey through a small, carefully researched collection:

**A song I love → something I can hear and understand → a person or idea behind it → another work worth exploring.**

A useful song page needs an accessible explanation, relevant credits, a few meaningful connections and visible sources. We do not need a vast catalog, a personalized algorithm or a complete theory course to validate that experience.

The listening journal and existing interface can support this direction. They should serve the central learning experience rather than determine it. Preserve the original interface as the Memphis Design Archive while the knowledge experience develops beside it.

## How we will know it helps

Early feedback should tell us whether listeners:

- Learned something they can explain in their own words.
- Noticed something new when they listened again.
- Followed a connection and understood why it was relevant.
- Trusted the explanation and could find its evidence.
- Wanted to return with another song or question.

Time spent in the app alone is not evidence of understanding.
